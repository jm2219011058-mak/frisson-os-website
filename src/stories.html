Human Should Not Simply Reach the Moon

— We Can Do Better

Published · July 25, 2026

On July 20, 1969, when Apollo 11 successfully landed on the Moon, humanity left its first footprint on another celestial body beyond its home planet. For decades, we have viewed this moment as a triumph of engineering — a technological miracle made possible by rockets, computation, materials science, and the collective intelligence of countless scientists and engineers.

Yet, as we began to rethink the boundaries of humanity’s future, we gradually realized that the question worth exploring is not whether we have the ability to reach the Moon, but whether we truly know how to live there once we arrive. Because reaching a place only demonstrates our ability to overcome distance; creating an environment capable of sustaining life represents something far greater — a civilization’s ability to truly understand its relationship with the world.

Looking back across the long arc of human history, we realize that the evolution of civilization has never been merely a story of technological advancement. It is, at its core, a continuous search for how humanity should inhabit the world. When our ancestors first entered caves, they were not simply seeking shelter from the forces of nature. In that moment, they began creating the first spaces shaped by human consciousness.

Ancient Egyptians built temples aligned with the heavens, the Greeks pursued harmony through proportion and order, and Renaissance civilizations created palaces, gardens, and spaces of art and thought because they believed that an environment was never simply designed to contain the human body — it also shaped human ideas, emotions, and ultimately, who we become.

However, as modern society evolved, we gradually changed the way we understood space. The industrial era taught us how to construct buildings at scale; the real estate era taught us how to measure land, area, and asset value; and the digital era shifted more and more of our attention toward screens and virtual worlds.

We have created increasingly powerful computing capabilities and artificial intelligence systems capable of processing enormous amounts of information, yet we continue to live within environments that cannot truly understand us.

A home knows how to turn on the lights, but it does not know what a person truly needs in the middle of the night. A building knows how to regulate temperature, but it does not understand the changes taking place within the human body. A city knows how to manage populations, but rarely understands how an individual life restores, creates, and grows within it.

As artificial intelligence enters its next phase, this contradiction becomes increasingly apparent. For the past decade, AI has primarily existed within the digital realm — learning languages, images, and the information created by humanity. But the next generation of intelligence is moving into the physical world. It will have a presence.

It will enter homes, cities, and increasingly complex human environments. Robots will need to understand the spaces they inhabit; an aging society will require new forms of environments designed to support human longevity; and when humanity eventually enters deserts, polar regions, deep oceans, or even the Moon, we will need more than temporary shelters. We will need entirely new systems of habitation capable of sustaining life over the long term.

We began to realize that the greatest challenge of the future is not simply making AI more intelligent, but enabling intelligence to truly understand the complex and subtle relationship between life and the environments that support it.

This is where FRISSON’s exploration of Sona began.

From the very beginning, we were not trying to create a smart home with more functions, because that still belongs to an outdated way of thinking — seeing space as a machine waiting to be controlled by humans. The question that truly fascinated us was this: what would happen if space were no longer a passive structure, but began to possess the ability to understand life?

If an environment could sense human rhythms, understand the relationship between the body and its surroundings, and continuously respond to human needs through light, air, sound, temperature, and spatial design, could a residence evolve from a static asset into a living system capable of accompanying human growth throughout life?

Through exploring this question, we gradually discovered that the true value does not lie in technology itself, but in the understanding that emerges when technology accompanies human life over time.

Like Pixar’s idea of the “invisible coin of perfection,” the details that audiences never consciously notice, yet determine whether a creation feels authentic, reveal that the greatest creations often come from places where no one is looking. The finest spaces of the future should not make people aware of sensors, algorithms, or complex systems operating behind the scenes.

Instead, all technology should disappear into the experience, leaving people with only one simple yet profound feeling: this place understands me; it knows how to support me; it helps me become closer to myself.

And it was precisely here that we began to recognize where FRISSON’s true long-term value may lie. We may not simply be building more buildings. We may be building a long-term knowledge system about how humanity lives. Because the most valuable data of the future may not come from the internet, but from the data generated through the continuous interaction between human life and physical environments.

The internet records what people express, what they search for, what they purchase, and what they discuss. But it cannot tell us how a person truly recovers within a certain environment, how a space influences sleep, health, emotions, and creativity, or how human beings can discover the right way to live across different climates, cultures, and even planetary environments.

This data cannot simply be obtained through the digital world. It must emerge from real physical spaces, accumulate through real time, and come from the long-term relationship between human beings and the environments they inhabit. This is Living Data — and it represents one of FRISSON’s most important areas of exploration.

It includes long-term patterns of human living behavior, the relationship between sleep, physiological states, environmental changes, and health outcomes, the continuous interaction between architecture and the human body, as well as the knowledge required to create new systems of habitation in extreme environments — from deserts and polar regions to the Moon and beyond.

Over the long term, this data will not only redefine residential environments; it may also become an essential training foundation for future AI healthcare, human-centered environmental design, robotics capable of understanding the physical world, and the next generation of intelligent systems.

This is why we believe that FRISSON’s most valuable asset in the future may not be the buildings themselves. Buildings will change. Materials will evolve. Forms will transform. But the understanding created through the shared journey between humans and spaces will leave behind something that cannot be replicated. This understanding cannot be purchased from the internet, nor can it be created through short-term experiments. It requires real people, real environments, and a relationship built through time.

Therefore, when we think about the future of human habitation, we are not simply thinking about how to create the next generation of luxury residences, nor are we merely thinking about how to build smarter homes. We are exploring a much deeper question: as humanity gradually becomes a civilization that extends beyond Earth, as we eventually move toward the Moon, Mars, and worlds beyond our current imagination, will we possess the wisdom to create, wherever we arrive, environments worthy of sustaining life?

Because reaching the Moon proves that humanity can arrive at the unknown.
But building permanent human habitats proves that humanity truly understands how to exist within the universe.

FRISSON is not simply exploring the future of residential living.
We are exploring a new form of civilization infrastructure — one that allows spaces to understand life, allows intelligence to enter the physical world, and allows humanity to continue creating places of belonging as we expand into an ever-growing universe.

What Made Me Impossible to Stop?

Take the Leap

Stealth Founder Mrs Jamie Z

September, 2026

The old physical world no longer matches the new digital identity.

Humans are not data; people need to stand on solid ground to know who they are.

I’m sitting in a room that doesn’t have any walls yet.

There’s no roof, no foundation, even the land itself doesn’t belong to anyone yet. But I can feel it.

It’s a strange feeling—you stand on an empty patch of land, yet you can already tell at what angle the light will fall at a certain hour, which direction the wind will pass through, and what kind of quiet hue this place will take on at dusk. You haven’t built anything, but you already know what the person living here will feel.

At the same time, we are rethinking how land itself should be valued—in the past, land value was mainly determined by location, area, planning, development intensity, nearby transactions, and expected rent and sale prices. Now Frisson wants to add a variable that the real estate market has rarely truly priced: who is willing to live here, and how many people are willing to gather in advance because of this place; when a Node already has a group of real Founding Residents before the land is acquired, their lifestyles, sensory preferences, shared interests, willingness to pay, and network relationships themselves begin to form a new demand signal, so land is no longer just raw material that developers buy first and then seek demand for, but may become a living asset jointly discovered, chosen, and driven to value realization by a group of future residents.

This is how Frisson begins.

Trust and Focus—the scarce qualities in a post-scarcity era.

I can’t imagine that Satoshi Nakamoto came up with the idea of inventing Bitcoin by studying TechCrunch.

On October 31, 2008, under the shadow of the global financial crisis, an unfamiliar name appeared on the P2P Foundation forum, releasing a nine-page paper.

No one paid attention, but he did it, stemming from an unprecedented idea. It hadn’t been verified in the mainstream tech world before, there was no data to support it, no market report to cite; he could only trust his own intuition, focus on writing it down, and send it out.

In a data-driven era, in an age where A/B tests and user personas can tell you everything, the scarcest things are precisely those that cannot be quantified—what you believe, and how much you are willing to pay for it.

Steve Jobs said years ago that the ‘Emotional Economy’ would replace the ‘Rational Economy’.

He criticized the consumer electronics industry for focusing only on technology, only knowing how to list parameters: how many MB of memory, how many megapixels in the camera, how many millimeters of thickness. ‘We make great computers’—that was the communication style of most companies.

But Apple’s way is: ‘Everything we do, we do to break through and innovate. We firmly believe in thinking differently.’

People don’t buy what you make; they buy why you make it.

That’s why we can’t stop creating.

From the Idea Stage, we have been committed to discovering invisible ‘creative forces’—they form the ‘Sensory Lab’—a visual artist from Venezuela. At 18, she emigrated to Spain alone. After the 2020 lockdown, she began to create. The world suddenly slowed down, and things usually obscured by daily life became clear: an empty room, the shifting light by the window in the afternoon, the quiet left behind after someone leaves, and those moments that never happened yet still remain in memory. She started to identify from the daily ‘absence’ those things worth recording. Five years later, her works appeared at Christie’s auctions and were exhibited in New York, Paris, Milan, Florence, and Lisbon, featured in Aesthetica magazine, and also displayed at SuperRare Gallery Soho.

But what truly attracted me was not these credentials, but the way she experiences the world. She listens to Ludovico Einaudi’s piano, Radiohead’s coolness, Joy Division’s undercurrent, and Novo Amor’s atmosphere, and then tries to ‘take out’ these things from the music. For her, a melody is not just a sound; it has color, temperature, distance, and even a kind of air that can enter a room. She translates melody into vision, and then further translates vision into the emotional tone of space. We later came to call this work Sensory Translation: Translating emotion, memory, and presence into sensory coordinates for space. What she does, in essence, is build a sensory language for space—giving emotion, memory, and presence a form that can be seen, heard, and touched.

Network before Architecture.

The Japanese Society of Kansei Engineering divides the creation of sensory value into three levels.

The first level is the five senses and feeling—people perceive the external world through sight, hearing, smell, touch, and taste. The second level is individuality and taste—the uniqueness and difference that products bring to consumers through design. The third level is content, art, and craftsmanship—satisfying consumers’ curiosity for knowledge and culturally nurturing them.

These three levels exactly correspond to what Frisson is doing.

Steve Jobs also said another thing that I often think about.

He said that Apple’s products are not just ‘things’. They are souvenirs of a certain spiritual temperament. They are layered emotional totems.

Start with emotional resonance, then deliver on that promise.

We haven’t built anything—at least not yet—but we are building something more solid than a house: ‘trust and focus’… A Soulful Living Network.

Play, or Change?

Which Sensory Identity feels like you?

Published · August 15, 2026

A 170-Million-View Article Didn’t Really Hit Because It Was About “Changing Your Life”

At the beginning of 2026, a long-form essay titled How to Fix Your Entire Life in 1 Day became a phenomenon on X.

Published by Dan Koe in January, the essay explored identity, goals, behavior, the subconscious, and the reconstruction of the self. Public figures reported at different points suggest that it eventually reached roughly 170 million impressions—and possibly more.

What it truly touched was not, “How can I become more disciplined?”

It was a far more unsettling question:

What if I have never become the person I wanted to be—not because I wasn’t trying hard enough, but because I have been using an old identity to live a life that was never truly mine?

One of Dan Koe’s central arguments is that we often try to change our behavior first, expecting our identity to follow.

But lasting change may happen in precisely the opposite direction:

Change who you are first, and behavior begins to follow naturally.

For perhaps the first time, “changing your life” stops being a productivity problem.

It becomes an identity problem.

And the answer may be far more physical than we imagine.

Not Moving Faster, But Seeing Another Map

That is why I am increasingly unconvinced that trying harder is, by itself, a sufficiently powerful competitive advantage.

If everyone is running on the same map, then of course speed matters.

But what happens when someone suddenly realizes:

There is a road beyond the map.

You need to be able to step outside the maze.

But simply standing outside it is not enough.

It requires a rare kind of dual vision:

the ability to see from above, and still know how to enter.

The Real Fork Never Announces Itself

In chess, there is a beautifully elegant tactic called a ‘Fork’.

A piece moves into what appears to be an ordinary position—and suddenly attacks two, or even several, important targets at once.

The brilliance is not in how quickly the piece moves.

It is in the fact that only a few people recognize what that position means.

Many of the moments that change the course of a life do not look like opportunities when they arrive.

They never light up a sign in front of you saying:

“This is the fork in your life.”

From Paris to Tokyo, from Hong Kong to New York, from the desert to the sea...

The places change.
The architecture changes.
The culture changes.

But how you recover within a space, how you create, how you find solitude, how you relate to others—what kind of light you love, what textures you respond to, what sounds you seek, what rhythms allow you to feel most alive—

these things can be understood.
They can be remembered.
And they can be awakened again.

Perhaps they can become a person’s:

extension of self.

That is what Soulful Living Network truly wants to explore.

Not giving people more addresses to own.

But giving a person’s way of living a continuity that can travel across the world.

Picked a Side Early

“The founders who scale are the ones who picked a side early.” —— Marc Andreessen

Published · August 11, 2026

Soulful Talking with Nikhil Chouguley

The founders who achieve real scale are those who chose their future early — before the answers were clear, before the market had formed consensus, when there was no proof they were right. They decided what they were willing to believe, and then committed their time, judgment, resources, and life to that direction, until what once seemed like a stubborn conviction slowly became reality.

Bricks and Wood

When people try to understand how wealth is actually created in the physical world, they often attribute value to what is visible: “You must have simply acquired bricks and timber.” But real value has never resided in the materials themselves — it resides in the act of reorganizing them. In expertise, imagination, tools, systems, craft, and countless invisible judgments. In how one understands materials, how one assembles them, and how one imposes new order and meaning upon them. Raw materials are almost always worth far less than what they become after they have been orchestrated. What is truly scarce is never the material itself — it is the ability to see a new arrangement, and to possess the will to make that vision real.

I Picked My Side

I chose to believe that the physical world still holds vast, unclaimed value waiting to be redefined — and that space is the largest, most complex, and most overlooked vessel for that transformation. Since I was eighteen, I have been drawn to how spaces hold memory and emotion — how they carry the weight of a person’s experience, and how they quietly shape who we become, often without us ever noticing. That fascination has not faded with time. It has only become clearer. Recently, it brought me before a world‑class family, where I had the opportunity to share early visions for the future of human environments.

The Value Is in the Orchestration

This has also led me to reconsider what a company’s core capability truly is. Its value does not necessarily lie in developing any single product — but in discovering, nurturing, and orchestrating a portfolio of creative assets and emotional connections, each with its own distinct life. In identifying people with singular perspectives, building long‑term partnerships and aligned incentives with them, translating characters, stories, cultural symbols, and aesthetic languages into experiences that people genuinely desire to own and inhabit, iterating by observing how people respond, expanding, maintaining, and refreshing intellectual property over time, and managing the allocation and combination of resources across different assets. This capability is closer to that of an operator of a creative‑asset investment portfolio than to a traditional product design company. It requires recognizing potential before value is visible, cultivating emotional resonance over time, and organizing what might otherwise remain fragmented into a system capable of generating new value continuously. Just as bricks and timber do not constitute the full value of a building, a character, an artist, a material, or a story is only raw material. What is truly scarce is the capacity to bring these elements together and allow them to produce something far greater than their sum.

The Canvas

I have long admired Travis for his intensity and conviction — his refusal to accept that an industry has already been defined, that its rules are fixed, and that the way things have always been done is the way they must continue to be done. He constantly questioned everything everyone else took for granted. I see the same spark in myself — but my canvas is not code, nor is it mobility. It is the physical world we inhabit. The relationships between space, material, culture, art, and the human environment — connections that have existed for a long time but have rarely been truly redesigned. What gets me up every morning is precisely the kind of problem worth dedicating a lifetime to: expanding what seems inexpandable, inventing futures that do not yet exist, beginning the search for answers before others even see the question — and doing it all with genuine curiosity, ambition, and joy.

Life Perceived by Space

Creative forces provide an aesthetic language. Frisson gives that language a life that can be sensed by the body. The true capability lies in the translation between these two realms: carrying a creator’s vision into a language that the physical world can bear — through material, texture, light, sound, scent, and atmosphere — allowing what once existed only on a canvas, in a film, in a piece of music, or in the imagination to enter real space, where it can be entered, touched, heard, breathed, and felt over time. This translation does not happen through instinct alone. It requires a methodology, a proprietary library of materials, and a system of standards that ensures creative intent survives the journey from concept through design, engineering, and construction to final completion. And what is truly worth building is precisely this invisible infrastructure beneath the work — because style can be imitated, products can be copied, even buildings can be reconstructed, but a system capable of continuously discovering creative forces, understanding their unique languages, translating them into reality, and scaling that value is something genuinely difficult to replicate.

A Founder’s Journal on Creativity, Space, and Human Meaning

— I Have Always Been Searching for Those the World Has Yet to Truly See

Published · August 2, 2026

The Creator and Solitude

When I was a child, I often dreamed of a place like ancient Athens — a small world where highly creative artists and leaders of society had known one another since childhood, where their relationships were direct, sincere, and deeply human.

Beyond ancient Athens, in Renaissance Florence, artists, architects, philosophers, and the creators of society did not need to navigate complicated networks to prove their value. They were often connected from an early age, and through years of interaction, they came to understand not only each other’s ideas, but also each other’s character, values, and pursuits.

An artist did not need to constantly explain his existence, because his work itself was his language. An architect did not need to chase every opportunity, because his creations had already become part of the community. A young creator could have the rare privilege of growing before being understood.

But in modern cities such as New York, London, and Paris, artists who aspire to recognition often find themselves having to attend endless cocktail gatherings, seeking the approval of committees and those who determine whether opportunities will be granted.

Those who receive opportunities are often not necessarily those with the deepest vision, but those who happen to encounter the right person at the right moment. As a result, they spend their days attending social events rather than remaining in their studios.

They are unable to devote enough energy to the solitude that true creation requires. They are unable to fully develop, transcend, and move beyond the styles and techniques they initially learned — the very styles and techniques that have already found acceptance within the market.

This is how a kind of fleeting art emerges.

An individual with a clever idea, yet without having undergone the long process of refinement, discipline, and experience, introduces a concept that is then either celebrated or criticized by the media.

Those who evaluate such creations often face the same limitation. They too have endless social commitments and insufficient time for deep research and genuine experience. Therefore, when confronted with something truly complex or entirely new, they find themselves unable to fully comprehend it.

A Creative Ecosystem that Has Disappeared

I Discovered Another Possibility of Creation Through Japanese Tea Ceremony

Later, in Japan, I had the privilege of observing many tea masters with different styles, and I began to understand how they achieved such a profound state of relaxation and how they expressed themselves with such effortless grace.

The ritual of tea ceremony seemed as though it had always been organically connected with the tea master himself. Within this ritual, he was able to naturally express, with extraordinary precision and profound presence, the culture embedded within the tradition of tea.

The feeling was almost impossible to describe.

It was like the feeling brought by a beautifully composed Japanese garden.

There, nature and art exist within the same language. They harmonize with one another, reflect one another, and affirm one another.

But as I continued to explore more deeply, I gradually realized that the true creation of tea ceremony was not the tea itself.

After Science, Humanity Became the Greatest Mystery

The German mathematician Leo Frobenius once pointed out that, in early human civilizations, the animal world first became a source of awe and imagination for humanity.

Those forms of life closest to human beings — with their strength, freedom, instinct, and unpredictability — inspired humanity with the desire to understand life through observation, imitation, and even the impulse to become one with other forms of existence.

Animals were not merely humanity’s neighbors.
They were also a mirror through which humanity began to understand itself.

Later, humanity discovered another form of mystery through the plant world and the miracle of the earth’s abundance.

A seed buried beneath the soil enters a silence resembling death, only to eventually give birth to new life. The cycle of seasons, the growth of all living things, and the renewal of nature revealed that death was not the end, but part of the continuation of life itself.

Later still, in the earliest civilizations of the ancient Near East, humanity gradually turned its attention toward the heavens.

The movements of the seven visible celestial bodies became an essential way for humanity to understand the order of the universe. The movement of the stars, the cycles of time, and the rhythm of the cosmos were given sacred meaning.

The myths surrounding resurrection, death, and rebirth, as well as the symbols and rituals associated with the divine, were ultimately humanity’s attempts to understand the mystery of its own existence.

But as technology gradually unveiled the laws of the physical world and helped us solve an increasing number of problems related to efficiency, function, and control, I began to realize that the true challenge facing future spaces may no longer be how to protect the human body, but how to respond to human existence itself.

Because what space ultimately encounters is a specific human being: a person carrying memories, emotions, wounds, desires, habits, and unfinished dreams.

Human beings have never existed as isolated individuals. We continuously create environments, and those environments continuously shape us in return.

The spaces in which we live are not merely backgrounds outside of life. They are active forces participating in the formation of our existence.

A city shapes the rhythm of a generation.
A room influences an individual’s emotional state.
A beam of light, a material, a sound, or a spatial arrangement can quietly transform the way a person perceives the world, the way they behave, and even the way they understand themselves.

Space does not merely record human existence.
It shapes the future of human existence.

This may be the true question that future architecture must confront.

It is no longer simply about answering:
“Where do people live?”

It is about responding to a deeper question:
“What kind of people can we become within the environments we inhabit?”

When space begins to influence human states, emotions, creativity, and even the way people understand themselves, architecture is no longer merely a physical structure.

It becomes a medium through which human experience itself is shaped.

This is why the future of space cannot be created through technology alone.

Because these questions ultimately belong to creation.
They belong to those who are able to understand the hidden depths of human experience.

Therefore, the rarest resource of the future may no longer be land, materials, or architectural capability.

It may be those creators who can understand life and transform that understanding into meaningful experiences.

They may be artists, architects, material explorers, spatial philosophers, or those who continuously explore the relationship between humanity and nature, the body, emotion, time, and consciousness.

They create a new way of perceiving.
A new relationship between humanity and the world.
A new understanding of what it means to exist as a human being in this world.

And this is why Frisson has begun searching for them.

I have always been searching for those the world has yet to truly see.
